Mockup 047 — Simulator progressive disclosure

The Simulator tab currently shows everything at once: the explainer, five quick-fill chips, ten form fields plus a "More fields" disclosure, and — after Simulate — the verdict card, the rule list with its filter controls, and the merge timeline with an open diff panel. This mock stages it into three layers matched to the user's journey: the ask (a minimal form), the answer (the verdict card, never hidden), and the evidence (rules and merge history as summary drawers, opened on demand). Research notes at the bottom.

Mockup — app tokens from packages/app/src/index.css; the drawers and the consumed-blocks line are interactive, the rest is static. Scenario: the 046 run — npm react 4.17.20 → 4.17.21, 2 of 714 rules matched, groupName changed.

2 · After Simulate — the answer, then evidence on demand

The verdict card is the summary layer and is never collapsed — hiding the primary answer is the classic progressive-disclosure failure. Everything below it becomes a summary drawer: one line that abstracts its content (counts, provenance, what changed), so the collapsed state still carries the scent. The consumed-blocks aside inside the card gets the same treatment — one line, expandable in place.

2A′ · The consumed-blocks line only when it earns its place

Confirmed 2026-07-26: Renovate's defaults define all seven update-type blocks on every config, and major/minor/patch are empty {} — so "consumed by flattening, none applied" is true on virtually every major/minor/patch simulation. A line that is always present is furniture, not signal (the same reasoning 022 used to drop no-op clauses from the verdict sentence). New rule: the verdict card shows the aside only when an AUTHORED block was consumed without applying — a block whose content differs from the defaults (someone wrote minor: { automerge: true }), detectable from the effective-config provenance the app already computes (winning layer ≠ defaults). It names only those blocks and says why they didn't apply. Default-only consumption renders nothing here — the timeline's flatten ⊘7 chip keeps the mechanics discoverable. With an authored block, the card gains:

⊘ Your minor block (automerge, from :automergeMinor) didn't apply — this is a patch update.

…and with updateType unset: "— no updateType is set; fill the version pair or pick one to see it apply."

3 · The scent survives the folding

Every cross-link that exists today keeps working by opening what it targets: the ledger's "step 2 of 2 →" and the aside's "see the flatten step →" open the merge drawer and select the stop; "2 of 714 rules matched" opens the rules drawer; a share link whose simStep points at a stop arrives with the merge drawer open. Nothing a link can reach is ever behind a closed drawer when the user lands on it.

Research notes — why these choices

Compiled 2026-07-26 from NN/g, Shneiderman, and the Carbon / Primer / GitLab Pajamas design systems. Each principle below names the design decision it produced.

  • Never gate the primary answer. Progressive disclosure works only when "everything users frequently need" stays up front — get the split wrong and the extra click plus the implied "this is unimportant" actively hurt. (NN/g, "Progressive Disclosure") → the verdict card is never collapsed; only the evidence layers fold.
  • Cap disclosure at 2 levels — "designs that go beyond 2 disclosure levels typically have low usability." (NN/g, ibid.) → 1A merges the two existing form tiers (visible-but-prefilled fields + "More fields") into ONE drawer; the results page is page → drawer, nothing deeper.
  • Cut before you hide. Field-minimized forms hit 78% one-try completion vs. 42% without. (NN/g, "Website Forms Usability") → the updateType select is not moved — it is REMOVED, replaced by the derived value as text with an override link (the 015 derivation made it redundant).
  • Chips must populate or replace the form, not sit beside it. Conditional revelation keeps workflows short only when the primary view depends on the selection. (Carbon, "Disclosures") → the quick-fill's writes land in the drawer whose summary line SHOWS the written values — chips and a ten-field form no longer compete at the same rank.
  • "Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand." (Shneiderman, "The Eyes Have It", 1996) → verdict (overview) → rules drawer with its filters (zoom/filter) → merge timeline and step diff (details), each stage entered by an action on the previous one instead of all rendered open.
  • Collapsed headers need a count or preview, not a bare label — hidden content with weak scent is simply missed (hidden navigation: 27% usage vs. 48% visible; "valuable content hidden under an accordion may be missed altogether"). (NN/g, "Hamburger Menus…"; "Accordions on Desktop") → the 047 signature: every drawer's summary line is an abstract of its content ("2 of 714 matched · config:recommended ×1 · repo config ×1"), computed from the result.
  • Keep a persistent status summary regardless of what's collapsed. GitHub's PR review keeps the viewed-count header while diffs collapse; Vercel keeps deploy status above collapsed build phases. (GitHub Docs; Vercel Docs) → the verdict card plays that role here.
  • A disclosure must not move or reset unrelated UI. (GitHub Primer, "Progressive Disclosure") → drawers are independent (opening one never folds another), they keep their state across re-simulations, and cross-links open what they target (section 3) rather than pointing into a closed drawer.
  • Tabs mean mutually exclusive; accordion rows mean secondary siblings. (Carbon; NN/g) → why 2A (drawers) is recommended over 2B (tabs): the rule list and the merge diff are complementary evidence a user reads together.
  • Use disclosure sparingly — fix the worst signal-to-noise, not every section. (Primer; GitLab Pajamas) → exactly three folds on the whole tab: the form's prefilled details, the rule list, the merge history.
